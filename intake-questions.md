# Twenty questions for a hiring manager

What to ask, why each one matters, and four commitments to walk out with. The starred questions (★) are the ones that change how the search gets run.

## Before anything else

**Bring two real profiles and make the hiring manager react to them.** Pull one candidate you think is a clear yes and one you think is borderline, and put them in front of the manager early. Ten minutes of reacting to real people calibrates you better than any answer to any question below.

## What does good actually look like (5 questions)

**1. Walk me through someone who has done this job well, here or anywhere. What made them good?**
Opens with the manager talking rather than you pitching. Everything said afterward calibrates against this answer. If they cannot name anyone, that is information too.

**2. ★ If you had to trade one off, which matters more: [skill A] or [skill B]?**
Example: would you rather hire a strong engineer who can mentor, or a strong mentor who can still ship? The posting and the manager may be solving for different roles. Better to find that out in the first meeting than in week six.

**3. What does this person actually do in their first ninety days?**
Tells you whether the seat is productive immediately or ramps through training first. That changes who you target and what you can honestly promise on a call.

**4. Do they own the outcome, or are they paired with someone?**
Whether you need someone who owns the result end to end or someone who supports a team lead. Different candidate, different pitch.

**5. Which part of the stack, product, or customer type will they spend most of their time on?**
For example, backend services vs. developer-facing tooling, or enterprise customers vs. open source users. If the work leans heavily one way, candidates with that background jump in value and the list gets re-ranked.

## Calibration: so you stop wasting their time (4 questions)

**6. ★ Show me a resume you would say yes to, and one you would say no to.**
Nothing else gets you inside a hiring manager's head this fast. It turns your next submission from a guess into a match.

**7. What is the one thing they must be able to do on day one?**
Gives you the question to open every screening call with, and tells you what the manager will not train, which is usually the more useful half.

**8. What makes you say no in the first ten minutes of a call?**
Their actual disqualifiers, which are almost never the ones written on the posting.

**9. ★ How hard is the [degree / years / certification] requirement?**
Find out their real tolerance before you send someone they were never going to hire. Hold the line unless they say otherwise.

## The constraints that shape the search (4 questions)

**10. ★ Is the location or onsite requirement genuinely fixed? Any flexibility at all?**
This one constraint often decides whether your list has thirty names or thirteen. If the same posting runs at the same pay in several cities, the company may be less rigid about geography than the manager sounds.

**11. If there are multiple seats, are they the same job?**
If two locations lean toward different work, they may be two searches and should be run that way.

**12. ★ What is your approval ceiling above the posted band?**
Get it in writing. If an exceptional candidate needs more than the band, you need that answer today, not during a counter-offer. While you are there, confirm the manager describes the package the same way you do (base, target variable, sign-on, equity). If they say it differently to a candidate, you lose them at offer.

**13. What have you lost candidates on before?**
Comp, speed, the panel, the company story. Whatever they name is the leak to design the process around.

## Process: what holds the timeline (4 questions)

**14. Who else interviews, in what order, and what is each person assessing?**
Lets you build the scorecard around the real panel so nobody ends up comparing different things. Also surfaces scheduling bottlenecks before they cost you a candidate.

**15. ★ What is your feedback turnaround on a submitted profile?**
Ask for 48 hours, including on a no. Passive candidates are employed and not looking, so silence is where you lose them, not rejection. This is the highest-value commitment you can get out of the meeting.

**16. How many candidates do you want in front of you before you decide?**
Sets your pipeline target and gives you the opening to be honest back. A small list supports roughly one offer with no redundancy. Several finalists at once needs a much larger pool.

**17. Has anyone interviewed for this already? What happened?**
Tells you whether the req is fresh or has a history of near-misses. The near-misses show where the real bar sits, not the written one.

## The questions people forget to ask (3 questions)

**18. Is there an internal candidate?**
Ask it plainly. If there is, you need to know how serious before you spend three weeks on outbound.

**19. ★ What is the biggest reason this req is still open?**
Sometimes the answer is sourcing. Often it is the panel, the comp band, or a decision nobody has made. Either way you learn what you are actually solving.

**20. What happens to your team if this seat is still empty in six months?**
Gives you their real urgency, which determines how hard you can push on decisions and whether they will fight for an exception when you need one.

## Say one thing about the market before you leave the room

Come with one piece of market intel, such as a competitor layoff, a hiring surge, or a pay-band shift in your talent pool, and say it out loud. It turns your request for fast feedback from a process ask into a commercial one, and it shows you are tracking their market rather than just working their req.

## Four commitments to walk out with

| Commitment | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Forty-eight hour feedback, including on a no | The highest-value thing they can give you, and the cheapest for them to agree to. |
| Two standing interview slots a week | Held whether or not you fill them. Scheduling friction is the drop-off you can least control. |
| Their panel named today: who, in what order, assessing what | So the scorecard is built around their process rather than retrofitted to it. |
| Their approval ceiling, in writing | Before a counter-offer, not during one. |
